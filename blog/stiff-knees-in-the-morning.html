Stiff knees in the morning: what helps

By the Calm Joints physio team · Updated October 27, 2026 · 8 min read

Stiff knees in the morning are common, especially after 45, and they often loosen once the joint has moved for a few minutes. If the stiffness lasts about half an hour or less and the knee aches more with use, guidelines treat that pattern as typical of osteoarthritis and put exercise, not rest, at the centre of care.[1][3] A short warm-up before you stand, then a walk you can repeat, is a sound first step while you sort out whether a virtual physio visit would help.

Why are my knees stiff when I wake up?

Overnight the knee barely bends. Fluid in the joint shifts, the muscles around the thigh and calf cool, and the first steps of the day ask a quiet joint to take full body weight. That is why the first trip to the kitchen can feel wooden, then easier by the time the kettle boils.

NICE guidance uses the length of that stiffness as part of a clinical picture. In people aged 45 or over, activity-related joint pain with no morning stiffness, or with stiffness that lasts no longer than 30 minutes, is enough to diagnose osteoarthritis without a routine X-ray.[1] Longer stiffness, especially if several joints are hot or swollen, is a different pattern and needs a clinician, not a stretch.

Other ordinary reasons the first steps hurt:

  • A sudden jump in walking, stairs, or kneeling the day before.
  • Sitting still in the evening, then lying still all night. Our piece on lower back pain from sitting covers the desk side of that same stillness.
  • A knee that already complains on stairs. If the sharp part is the descent, knee pain going down stairs is the closer match.
  • Cooler rooms. Canadian autumn and winter do not cause osteoarthritis, but a cold bedroom can make the first bends feel stiffer until you move.

Is morning stiffness a sign the joint is wearing out?

Stiffness is a symptom, not a forecast. Osteoarthritis is a whole-joint condition, and cartilage change on a scan does not map neatly onto how you feel. NICE is clear that management should follow symptoms and function, and that imaging is not needed to confirm a typical case.[1]

The Arthritis Society Canada notes that too little activity can make osteoarthritis symptoms worse, because unused muscles get weaker and the joint gets less of the movement that keeps it comfortable.[7] That is the opposite of the old advice to save the knee by sitting more. Light or moderate activity, including walking and range-of-motion work, is part of how people stay able to do ordinary things.

What the research says

Land-based exercise is the treatment with the most consistent support.

A Cochrane review led by Fransen pooled 44 trials and found that exercise reduced knee pain by about 12 points on a 0 to 100 scale right after treatment, and improved physical function by about 10 points. Smaller gains in pain were still there two to six months after the formal program ended. That is a moderate change, not a cure, and it is high-quality evidence.[2]

The 2019 OARSI guideline on non-surgical care gives a strong recommendation for structured land-based exercise programs for knee osteoarthritis.[3] A later OARSI rehabilitation consensus says the dose should be enough to matter, encourages exercise two or more times a week, and favours a long-term habit over short bursts that stop when the knee feels better.[4]

A 2025 BMJ network meta-analysis of 217 randomised trials and 15,684 people compared exercise types. Aerobic exercise had the highest chance of being the best option across pain, function, gait, and quality of life, with moderate-certainty evidence for large short- and mid-term pain improvements. Strengthening and mixed programs also improved function. Safety reporting was thin, and the trials did not show a clear difference in harms between exercise and control.[5]

The American College of Rheumatology and Arthritis Foundation guideline, written by Kolasinski and colleagues, strongly recommends exercise for knee osteoarthritis. Walking, strengthening, and neuromuscular work all sit inside that recommendation.[6] NICE says the same in plainer language: offer therapeutic exercise tailored to the person, including local strengthening and general aerobic fitness, and warn that pain can rise a little when exercise starts. Regular practice is still expected to help pain, function, and quality of life.[1]

Virtual care is not a weaker version of that advice. A 2024 meta-analysis of telehealth-supported exercise for knee osteoarthritis (23 studies) found less pain and better function, activity, and quality of life than control conditions.[8] A 2025 BMJ Open review of digital physiotherapy reported moderate-quality evidence for better pain and function, including video visits.[9] That is the kind of visit Calm Joints offers from home.

Does walking help a stiff knee, or make it worse?

For most people with this pattern, walking helps, if the dose fits. Arthritis Society Canada points to walking as one of the useful options, and suggests building toward 150 minutes a week of moderate activity in bouts of 10 minutes or more, if you can tolerate it.[7] You do not need to start there. Ten easy minutes after the morning loosen-up beats a heroic weekend hike followed by three days on the couch.

If the hip is the joint that argues on a walk, our post on whether walking is good for hip arthritis goes further on that question. For the knee, use a flat route, supportive shoes, and a pace where you could still talk. Ice and hills can wait until the flat walk feels ordinary.

Pain during the walk that settles within a day is common when you begin. Pain that climbs through the walk, or a knee that swells and stays hot into the next morning, is a signal to shorten the bout and get it assessed.

Try this today

These are gentle starters, not a program. Stop if pain sharpens, the knee gives way, or swelling blooms. A physio can change the dose to fit your knee.

1. Bend-and-straighten before you stand

Sit on the edge of the bed. Slide one heel back so the knee bends as far as is comfortable, then slide it forward until the knee is straight. Move slowly for one to two minutes each side. This is a range-of-motion drill, the type Arthritis Society Canada links with less pain and stiffness. Stop if the knee locks or the pain jumps.

2. Sit-to-stand from a firm chair

Use a kitchen chair, feet flat, knees roughly over the ankles. Lean the chest forward a little and stand up, then sit back down with control. Start with 5 slow repeats. Rest. If that felt manageable the next day, add a few reps, not a second workout. Stop if the kneecap feels like it will not hold you.

3. A ten-minute flat walk

After the two drills, walk a flat block or a mall corridor. Keep the stride short at first. If the knee is stiffer at the end than at the start, cut the walk in half tomorrow rather than skipping it. OARSI’s exercise group favours a repeatable dose over an occasional long one.

4. A warm start, not a hot soak as treatment

A warm shower before the drills can make the first bends easier. It does not replace the movement. Skip heat if the knee is newly hot, red, or swollen.

Physio tip: None of these replaces a program matched to your knee. They are safe starters a physio can progress.

When to see a physio

Book a visit if morning stiffness is still running your first hour after a couple of weeks of gentle movement, if stairs or a short walk are shrinking, or if you are unsure how hard to push. A virtual physio visit can check the pattern, set a dose you can keep, and tell you what is fine to feel and what is not.

Calm Joints visits are virtual, with registered physiotherapists, for people across Canada. Book a virtual visit at calmjoints.org. Many extended health plans in Canada cover physiotherapy, including virtual visits. Check your booklet. OHIP does not generally pay for community physiotherapy in the same way those plans do, so the plan question is worth a look before you assume it is out of pocket.

Get urgent care the same day, and do not wait for a physio booking, if:[10]
  • You cannot put weight on the leg, or the knee has changed shape after a twist or fall.
  • The knee is hot, red, and badly swollen, especially with fever or feeling shivery. Infection in a joint needs emergency care.
  • The knee locks and will not unlock, or it gives way again and again.
  • Stiffness lasts many hours, several joints are swollen, or you have had this for months without being assessed. Inflammatory arthritis is a medical question, not an exercise question.
  • Calf swelling and pain after travel or illness, which can be a clot rather than a stiff knee.
In an emergency, or if you are very unwell, call 911.

A calm week, not a perfect week

You do not need to erase morning stiffness to have a better month. The research target is a knee that hurts less and works better, built by exercise you repeat. Start with the bed bends and a short walk.

Frequently asked questions

How long should morning knee stiffness last?

In the pattern NICE uses for osteoarthritis, stiffness lasts no longer than about 30 minutes. Stiffness that runs for hours, or that comes with hot swollen joints, should be checked rather than stretched through. [1]

Should I rest a stiff knee in the morning?

A minute to warm up is fine. A morning of rest usually is not. Guidelines put therapeutic exercise and staying active at the centre of osteoarthritis care, and Arthritis Society Canada notes that too little activity can make symptoms worse. [1][7]

Do I need an X-ray for stiff knees?

Not always. NICE says a typical story in someone 45 or older can be diagnosed without routine imaging. Imaging matters more after injury, when the knee looks deformed, or when symptoms do not fit the usual pattern. [1]

What exercise is best for stiff knees?

Aerobic work such as walking ranked highest across outcomes in a 2025 BMJ network meta-analysis, and strengthening helps function too. The best option is the one you can repeat twice a week or more, progressed with a physio if pain or swelling keeps rising. [4][5]

Can a virtual physio visit help morning knee stiffness?

Research on telehealth-supported exercise and on digital physiotherapy for knee osteoarthritis shows improvements in pain and function. A visit is a good fit if you want a dose set for your knee, your building, and your week, without travelling to a clinic. [8][9]
