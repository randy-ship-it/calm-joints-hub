Is cycling good for knee arthritis? What helps

By the Calm Joints physio team · Updated November 6, 2026 · 7 min read

Research suggests yes: cycling is a low-impact option linked to less knee pain and arthritis in large studies, and exercise in general reduces pain and improves function for many people with knee osteoarthritis.[1][2] Start gently, check your setup, and stop if pain worsens sharply. A registered physio can help tailor it. This is educational information, not personal medical advice.

Why does cycling help knees with arthritis?

Cycling keeps the knees moving through a smooth range without the repeated impact of walking or running. Your body weight is supported by the seat, so compressive load through the joint is lower than on the ground. The circular motion can also encourage the joint fluid that lubricates the knee, and it builds the muscles around the knee that help share the load.

Arthritis Society Canada lists cycling (outdoor or stationary) among arthritis-friendly activities for this reason.[5] Guidelines from NICE and OARSI treat therapeutic exercise, including aerobic options like cycling, as a core treatment for osteoarthritis, alongside education and weight management when appropriate.[3]

What the research says

  • A 2024 observational study using Osteoarthritis Initiative data looked at more than 2,600 adults (average age about 64). People who reported any history of bicycling were less likely to have frequent knee pain (adjusted risk ratio 0.83), radiographic osteoarthritis (0.91), and symptomatic radiographic osteoarthritis (0.79) than those who never cycled. The authors noted this does not prove cause and effect, but it supports encouraging cycling for knee health.[1]
  • A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of stationary cycling trials in people with knee osteoarthritis found it reduced pain (about 13 points on a 0 to 100 scale) compared with no exercise. Benefits for stiffness, daily activities, and quality of life were smaller and not always clearly clinically important.[4]
  • The most recent Cochrane review of exercise for knee osteoarthritis (updated 2024) found low- to moderate-certainty evidence that exercise can improve pain by roughly 13 points on a 0 to 100 scale compared with usual care or limited education, with benefits also seen for physical function. No single type of exercise clearly outperformed the others overall; the best one is the one you can keep doing.[2]
  • A 2025 BMJ network meta-analysis of 217 trials suggested aerobic exercise (examples included walking, cycling, and swimming) had the highest probability of being the best option among exercise types for short- and mid-term pain and function in knee osteoarthritis.[6]
  • NICE guidance (NG226) recommends therapeutic exercise tailored to the person, and notes that some increase in pain is common when starting and that consistent practice tends to help over time.[3]
  • Systematic reviews of telerehabilitation show it can produce similar improvements in pain and function to in-person care for many musculoskeletal conditions when guided by a physiotherapist.[7]

How should I set up a bike if I have knee arthritis?

Seat height matters. Arthritis Society Canada advises adjusting so your knee is only slightly bent when the pedal is at its lowest point.[5] Too much bend can increase stress; reaching too far can strain other areas. On a stationary bike, start with little or no resistance. Recumbent models can feel easier on the back for some people.

Outdoor cycling works well on smooth paths. In Canadian winters, indoor stationary bikes keep the habit going. Begin with short sessions (5 to 10 minutes) and build gradually. If you notice sharp pain, swelling that does not settle, or a new locking sensation, ease off and check with a professional.

Try this today

These are simple starting points. Stop if pain rises sharply, the knee swells noticeably, or you feel unsteady. They are not a full program.

1. Seat-height check (stationary or outdoor)

Sit on the bike with one pedal at the bottom. Your knee should have a small, comfortable bend (roughly 10 to 15 degrees). Adjust the seat up or down until it feels right. Pedal slowly for 1 to 2 minutes at very low resistance. Stop if you feel sharp front-of-knee pain or grinding that increases.

2. Short, easy spin

After a 2-minute warm-up of gentle pedalling, ride 5 to 10 minutes at a pace where you can still talk. Keep resistance low. Cool down for 1 to 2 minutes. Stop if pain climbs above your usual level and stays up, or if swelling appears during or right after.

3. Seated knee extension (strength support)

Sit on a firm chair, foot on the floor. Slowly straighten one knee until the leg is almost straight, hold 2 to 3 seconds, lower with control. Do 8 to 10 on each side if comfortable. This builds the quadriceps that help control the kneecap and share load. Stop if you feel sharp pain under the kneecap or the knee feels unstable.

4. Movement break after sitting

Before you stand from a chair, gently bend and straighten the knee a few times in the air or with the foot lightly on the floor. Then stand slowly. This can reduce the first-step stiffness many people notice.

Physio tip: A registered physiotherapist can show you how these fit your knees and progress them safely.

When to see a physio

See a physiotherapist if knee pain limits walking, stairs, or daily tasks for more than a couple of weeks, keeps returning after activity, or makes you avoid movement. Early guidance on setup, strength, and pacing often helps more than waiting.

Calm Joints visits are virtual, with registered physiotherapists, for people across Canada. Book a virtual visit at calmjoints.org. Many extended health plans cover physiotherapy, including virtual visits. Research on telerehabilitation for musculoskeletal conditions shows comparable outcomes to in-person care for pain and function when a clinician guides the plan.[7] For more, see does virtual physiotherapy work.

Seek same-day medical care, and do not wait for a routine physio slot, if you have:
  • Inability to bear weight, or a knee that looks deformed after injury.
  • A hot, red, swollen knee with fever or feeling unwell (possible infection).
  • Locking that prevents you from straightening the knee.
  • Sudden severe swelling or giving way after trauma.
In an emergency, call 911.

Frequently asked questions

Is cycling better than walking for knee arthritis?

Both are low-impact aerobic options that research supports. Cycling often feels easier on sensitive knees because body weight is supported. Many people do well combining short walks with cycling. The 2025 BMJ analysis highlighted aerobic exercise in general as particularly helpful. [6]

Will cycling make my knee arthritis worse?

Evidence points the other way for most people when intensity is matched to symptoms. The lifetime cycling study linked more cycling to lower rates of pain and arthritis signs. Start low, progress slowly, and adjust if pain increases and stays up. [1]

How often should I cycle?

Guidelines recommend aiming toward 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity per week for general health, broken into short bouts if needed. For knees, consistency matters more than long sessions. A physiotherapist can help set a realistic dose. [2][3]

Can I cycle after a knee replacement?

Many people return to stationary cycling as part of rehab once cleared by their surgical team. Timing and setup vary. Check with your surgeon or physio before starting.

Does virtual physio work for this?

Yes for many people. Reviews of telerehabilitation show it can match in-person results for musculoskeletal pain and function while being more convenient. Calm Joints offers virtual visits with registered physiotherapists. [7]

What if I get pain under the kneecap when I pedal?

This can relate to load on the patellofemoral joint. Lower the resistance, raise the seat slightly if it is too low, and shorten sessions. Persistent pain is a good reason to book a physio visit for a tailored check.
