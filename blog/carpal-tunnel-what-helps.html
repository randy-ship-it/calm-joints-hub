Carpal Tunnel: What Helps

By the Calm Joints physio team · Updated November 17, 2026 · 7 min read

Carpal tunnel syndrome happens when the median nerve is squeezed at the wrist, causing numbness, tingling or pain in the thumb and first fingers, often worse at night. Research supports starting with a neutral night-time wrist splint, education, activity changes and physiotherapy for mild to moderate cases.[2][3][5] Progressive weakness or constant numbness needs timely professional assessment.

What is carpal tunnel syndrome?

The carpal tunnel is a narrow passageway on the palm side of the wrist, formed by the carpal bones underneath and a strong ligament across the top. The median nerve and the tendons that bend your fingers run through it.

When pressure inside the tunnel rises, the median nerve can be compressed. This is carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS), the most common entrapment neuropathy. Prevalence in the general population is estimated at roughly 1% to 5%, with higher rates in women, especially between about 40 and 60 years of age.

The little finger is almost never affected, because a different nerve supplies it. If your little finger is numb or tingling along with the others, the problem is more likely elsewhere.

What are the common symptoms?

  • Numbness or pins and needles in the thumb, index, middle and half of the ring finger.
  • Symptoms that wake you at night or are worse first thing in the morning.
  • Temporary relief from shaking or changing the position of the hand.
  • Aching in the wrist or forearm.
  • Later, clumsiness with buttons, jars or keys, or weakness of thumb movement.

Symptoms can come and go for months or years. Both sides are often affected, though one side usually starts first.

What can contribute to it?

CTS usually has more than one cause. Contributors include sustained or repetitive wrist bending (some work tasks, driving, sleeping positions), higher body weight, pregnancy, an underactive thyroid, rheumatoid arthritis, a previous wrist fracture, and being female and middle-aged. High keyboard use alone has not been shown to be a strong cause in the best available reviews. Overall posture, force and recovery time matter more.

What the research says

Evidence for non-surgical care is mixed and often rated low or moderate certainty, which is worth stating plainly.

  • A Cochrane review of exercise and mobilisation found only limited, very low-quality evidence of benefit. The authors concluded that people who prefer these approaches should be told about the limited evidence, and decisions should rest on clinician expertise and patient preference.[1]
  • A Cochrane review of splinting found limited evidence overall. Night-time splinting may increase the chance of reporting overall improvement in the short term, though effects on specific symptoms and function are small or uncertain.[2]
  • A systematic review of physiotherapy and sports-therapy treatments for mild to moderate CTS reported positive effects on symptoms from manual therapy, mobilisation, massage, kinesiotaping and yoga. In some studies manual therapy gave faster improvement than surgery and comparable longer-term results.[3]
  • Clinical guidelines support a neutral-position wrist orthosis worn at night as a first-line option for short-term relief, with education about aggravating postures and activity modification.[5]
  • Surgery is more likely to give lasting improvement than splinting alone for some people, especially when symptoms are severe or have not responded to other care. Shared decision-making is essential.[6]

In short: many people with mild to moderate symptoms improve with night splinting, education, activity changes and guided physiotherapy. Results vary, and no single exercise program is proven best.

Try this today

These are simple, low-risk habits. Stop anything that increases numbness, tingling or pain, and do not force range.

1. Keep the wrist neutral

Neutral means straight, not bent up or down and not twisted. When typing or using a mouse, keep the wrist roughly in line with the forearm. Set chair and desk height so your elbows sit near 90 degrees and shoulders stay relaxed. Take a short movement break every 20 to 30 minutes.

2. Gentle median nerve glide

Sit or stand tall with your arm by your side, elbow bent, palm up. Slowly extend the wrist and fingers while gently tilting your head away, then return. Stay in a comfortable range for 5 to 10 slow repetitions. Stop if symptoms increase.

3. Neutral position at night

Many people sleep with their wrists bent. A soft neutral wrist support worn only at night can reduce overnight pressure for some. Follow the product guidance and remove it if it causes pressure or skin irritation.

4. Manage load

Reduce sustained gripping and extreme wrist positions where you can, and alternate tasks. Strength work for the whole arm and shoulder, guided by a physiotherapist, is often more useful than wrist stretches alone.

Physio tip: If symptoms have not settled after a few weeks of steady changes, or they interrupt sleep or daily tasks, an assessment is the logical next step.

When to see a physio

A registered physiotherapist can check whether your symptoms fit a carpal tunnel pattern, rule out other causes such as the neck, and build a plan that matches your severity and goals. Virtual visits work well for education, exercise progression, posture advice and follow-up for many hand and wrist problems. Many extended health plans in Canada cover physiotherapy, including virtual visits. Book a virtual visit at calmjoints.org.

Seek same-day medical care if you have:
  • Rapidly worsening numbness or weakness over days.
  • Signs of infection: redness, heat, fever or a recent wound near the wrist.
  • Symptoms that began after a fracture, dislocation or significant trauma.
  • Visible wasting of the muscle at the base of the thumb, or clear loss of thumb strength.
  • Symptoms that involve the neck, the whole arm or the little finger, which can point to a different nerve problem.
In an emergency, call 911.

Frequently asked questions

Does typing cause carpal tunnel syndrome?

High keyboard use by itself has not been strongly linked as a cause in major reviews. Overall load, wrist posture, force and recovery time appear more relevant. A good workstation setup and regular movement still help most people. [3]

Will a night splint fix carpal tunnel?

Night-time neutral splinting may help some people report overall improvement in the short term. Effects on specific symptoms are often modest and can be temporary. It is a low-risk first step for many, not a guaranteed cure. [2][5]

Can physiotherapy replace surgery?

For mild to moderate CTS, physiotherapy approaches such as education, activity modification, manual therapy and exercise help many people avoid or delay surgery. Severe or progressive cases, or those that do not improve, often do better with surgical release. A proper assessment guides the choice. [3][6]

How long does carpal tunnel last?

Some mild cases improve within weeks to months with conservative care. Others persist or progress over years. Early attention to night position, load and guided rehab improves the odds of recovery. [4]

Is physiotherapy for carpal tunnel covered in Canada?

Most extended health benefit plans cover physiotherapy, and virtual care is usually eligible under the same benefits. OHIP does not generally cover routine private physiotherapy. Check your plan for details and any referral requirements. [4]
