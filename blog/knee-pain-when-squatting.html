Knee Pain When Squatting: What Helps

By the Calm Joints physio team · Updated November 27, 2026 · 7 min read

Most knee pain when you squat is not a sign that your knee is damaged. It is usually the front of the knee taking more load than it is ready for right now.[1] Research shows that graded exercise, especially strengthening the hip and thigh, plus small changes to how you squat, helps most people reduce pain and move with more confidence.[1][2][3]

Why does my knee hurt when I squat?

The most common reason is patellofemoral pain, meaning the joint behind your kneecap is irritated. It often feels like an ache around or behind the kneecap that gets worse with squatting, going down stairs, or sitting for a while. It usually starts without a single injury; instead, the load on the knee has gone up faster than the tissues can handle.[1]

Other possibilities include irritation of the tendon just below the kneecap or, in some people, early osteoarthritis changes. A physiotherapist can help sort this out. The same principles help across these common causes: build the capacity of the muscles around the knee and manage how much load you place on it.

What the research says

  • The JOSPT clinical practice guideline recommends exercise therapy focused on the hip and knee for patellofemoral pain. Combined hip and knee strengthening reduces pain and improves function better than knee exercises alone in the short, medium and long term.[1]
  • A Cochrane review found that exercise therapy may produce clinically important reductions in pain and better function for people with patellofemoral pain, although the overall quality of evidence was low.[2]
  • A living network meta-analysis found education plus exercise (sometimes with supportive measures such as taping) more effective than waiting to see what happens.[3]
  • Physiotherapists who treat front-of-knee pain emphasize clear education, keeping pain mild, and progressive loading. Squatting can be part of the solution once the movement is adjusted so the knee is not overloaded.[4] For people who also have osteoarthritis features, first-line care still centres on education, exercise and activity changes.[5]
  • Remotely delivered physiotherapy has been shown to be as effective as face-to-face physiotherapy for musculoskeletal conditions.[6]

How can I squat without making it worse?

You do not have to stop squatting forever. Small changes often let you keep moving while the knee calms down.

  • Limit how deep you go. Stop before the pain rises sharply. Many people find a partial squat, or a sit-to-stand from a higher surface, more comfortable at first.
  • Keep your knees roughly in line with your feet. If they collapse inward, slow down and push the floor away evenly through the whole foot.
  • Stand up through your heels and mid-foot rather than letting the knees shoot far forward.
  • Build strength around the knee with exercises that do not aggravate it, then gradually bring back deeper or heavier squats.

Pain that stays under about 3 to 5 out of 10 during an activity and settles quickly afterward is often acceptable while you rebuild capacity. Sharp, increasing or lasting pain is a signal to ease off.[4]

Try this today

These are simple starting points. Stop if pain rises above mild or lasts more than a short time after you finish.

1. Partial sit-to-stand

Sit on a firm chair with feet flat and hip-width apart. Lean forward slightly and stand up, using your hands on the chair only if needed. Lower back down slowly and with control. Aim for 8 to 10 repetitions, and repeat once if it feels okay. Stop if the front of the knee becomes more than mildly uncomfortable.

2. Side-lying hip lift

Lie on your side with knees bent and feet together. Keep your feet touching and lift the top knee a few inches without rolling your pelvis backward. Hold briefly and lower slowly. Do 10 to 12 on each side. Stop if you feel pinching in the hip or more knee pain.

3. Short wall sit

Stand with your back against a wall and feet about a step out. Slide down until your knees are bent at a comfortable angle; you do not need to reach 90 degrees. Hold for 10 to 20 seconds, then stand up. Repeat 3 to 5 times. Stop if pain builds or your form collapses.

Physio tip: A higher chair or box is a simple way to keep squatting while the knee settles. Lower the target an inch at a time as it gets easier.

When to see a physio

See a registered physiotherapist if the pain has lasted more than a couple of weeks, is stopping you from everyday activities, or you are unsure how to progress safely. A physiotherapist can assess how you move, guide the right exercises, and help you get back to the things you want to do. Virtual visits work well for this kind of problem.

Seek same-day medical care, and do not wait for a routine physio slot, if you have:
  • Significant swelling, locking or giving way after an injury.
  • Pain that wakes you at night and is not eased by changing position.
  • Fever, redness or warmth around the knee with the pain.
  • Sudden inability to put weight on the leg.
In an emergency, call 911.

Frequently asked questions

Is squatting bad for my knees?

Not for most people. Squatting is a normal movement and can be part of rehab. If it hurts, modify the depth and load rather than avoiding the movement entirely. [4]

Should I stop squatting completely?

Usually no. Complete rest can make the knee more sensitive. Graded activity and strength work tend to help more than stopping altogether. [1]

How long does it take to feel better?

Many people notice improvement within a few weeks of consistent exercise and load management. Building full capacity can take longer. [2]

Do I need imaging?

Most of the time, no. A clinical assessment is enough for typical front-of-knee pain. Imaging is more useful if there are red flags or the pain is not improving as expected. [1]

Can a virtual physio help?

Yes. Remotely delivered physiotherapy has been shown to be as effective as face-to-face care for musculoskeletal conditions. [6]
