Pain at the Base of the Thumb: What Helps

By the Calm Joints physio team · Updated November 24, 2026 · 6 min read

Pain at the base of the thumb is often osteoarthritis of the carpometacarpal (CMC) joint. Education, joint protection, exercises and a short-term thumb support can reduce pain and improve hand function for most people.[1][2][3] A virtual physio visit helps you find the right combination for your hands and daily tasks.

Many people notice it first when opening jars, turning keys or pinching. The joint sits where the thumb meets the wrist, and everyday gripping places high loads on it. You do not have to stop using your hands. Small changes in how you grip and move often make a clear difference.

Why does the base of the thumb hurt?

The CMC joint at the base of the thumb is mobile so you can bring the thumb to the fingers, but it is not especially stable. Over time the cartilage can thin and the ligaments can loosen. This is more common after age 40 and more frequent in women. Osteoarthritis commonly affects the base of the thumb, the middle knuckles and the end joints of the fingers.[4]

Grasping multiplies force at this joint: one kilogram at the tip of the thumb can mean roughly twelve kilograms of load at the base. Repeated high loads without recovery contribute to symptoms. Previous injury or family history can play a role, but many people develop it without a clear trigger.

What does base-of-thumb arthritis feel like?

Typical signs include aching or sharp pain at the fleshy base of the thumb, especially with pinch or twist. The area may look squared or swollen. Pinch strength drops, so jars and keys become harder. Morning stiffness usually eases within an hour. This differs from De Quervain’s tendinopathy, which tends to hurt more on the side of the wrist with thumb movement.[7]

What the research says

  • A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis found that physical therapy approaches produced clinically worthwhile reductions in pain (roughly 3 points on a 0 to 10 scale) and improvements in hand function.[1]
  • A 2021 randomized trial of 204 people showed that education plus a thumb splint, hand exercises and topical anti-inflammatory gel improved hand function more than education alone at 6 and 12 weeks.[2]
  • The British Society for Surgery of the Hand (2023) recommends a stepwise approach: supported self-management first, then a splint, then injection only if needed. Surgery is reserved for cases that do not improve.[3]
  • European hand osteoarthritis recommendations support orthoses for the thumb base, exercise, and topical anti-inflammatories.[6]

Effects are usually modest to moderate. Best results come from combining education, joint protection, progressive exercise and, when helpful, a supportive orthosis. Benefits fade if the strategies stop.

Joint protection that actually helps

Change the load rather than stop activity. Hold jars and bottles with the whole hand or both hands instead of a tight pinch. Slide or tip heavy pots instead of lifting them by the handle. Build up tool handles with foam tubing or grippers. Wear a thumb-supporting orthosis during aggravating tasks such as gardening, driving or opening containers for a few weeks, then reassess.[5]

Heat from warm water or a warm cloth can ease stiffness before activity. Cold can calm a flare. Topical anti-inflammatory gel is often first-line medication advice; check with a pharmacist or physician if you have other health conditions.[6]

Try this today

These are starting points only. Keep each movement smooth and comfortable, and stop if pain rises above mild or lasts after the set.

1. Gentle thumb opposition

Rest your hand on a table. Slowly touch the tip of the thumb to the tip of each finger in turn, then open the web space. Repeat 5 to 8 times. Stop if you feel sharp pain or the joint catching.

2. Soft-ball squeeze

Hold a soft foam ball or rolled towel. Gently squeeze and release, keeping the thumb in a comfortable open position rather than collapsing into the palm. Aim for 8 to 10 slow repetitions.

3. Thumb stretch

Gently extend the thumb away from the palm, supporting it with the other hand if needed. Hold 5 to 10 seconds, 3 to 5 times. Stop if the base of the thumb pinches.

Physio tip: Use “C” or “O” grips and wider handles so the load spreads across the whole hand instead of a tight pinch.

When to see a physio

Book a visit when pain limits daily tasks, when you want a tailored exercise and joint-protection plan, or when you are unsure whether a support is worth trying. A physio can screen for other causes, teach the most useful grips for your tasks (kitchen, garden, computer, sport), and progress your exercises. Many people improve enough with guided self-management that they never need injection or surgery.

Seek same-day medical care, and do not wait for a routine physio slot, if you have:
  • Sudden severe swelling after an injury.
  • Fever with a hot, red joint.
  • Inability to move the thumb after a fall or twist.
  • Numbness or weakness that spreads into the arm.
In an emergency, call 911.

Frequently asked questions

Is pain at the base of the thumb always arthritis?

No. It is the most common cause after age 40, but tendon irritation, ligament strain or referral from the neck or wrist can produce similar symptoms. A physio assessment helps sort this out. [4]

Will a thumb brace make my thumb weaker?

Short-term use during aggravating activities is recommended in guidelines and does not appear to cause lasting weakness. Long-term continuous wear without exercise is less ideal. [3][6]

Can exercise make thumb arthritis worse?

Appropriately dosed exercise is supported by evidence and guidelines. Sharp or rapidly worsening pain is a signal to reduce load or get guidance, not a reason to stop all movement. [1]

Does OHIP cover physiotherapy for this?

In Ontario, OHIP covers physiotherapy only for specific groups. Most working-age adults use extended health benefits, and coverage varies by plan.

When is surgery considered?

Only after a thorough trial of non-surgical care has not provided enough relief. The most common procedure is trapeziectomy, and recovery takes months. [3]
