Is Physiotherapy Good for Arthritis? What Helps

By the Calm Joints physio team · Updated November 28, 2026 · 7 min read

Yes. Research and major guidelines show that physiotherapy built around tailored exercise, education and self-management helps reduce pain and improve function for people with arthritis, especially osteoarthritis of the knee and hip.[1][2][3] It does not reverse joint changes, but it is a core first-line treatment that many people find practical and effective, including through virtual visits.

More than six million Canadians live with arthritis, and most have osteoarthritis.[4] The fear that movement will wear the joint out is common, yet the opposite is true when exercise is done thoughtfully. A registered physiotherapist can show you how to load the joint in ways that build strength without flaring symptoms.

What is arthritis, and why does movement matter?

Arthritis is an umbrella term for conditions that affect joints. Osteoarthritis is the most common type in Canada. It involves changes throughout the joint, including cartilage, bone, the joint lining and the surrounding muscles, and typically brings activity-related pain and short-lived stiffness. Inflammatory types such as rheumatoid arthritis involve the immune system and need medical management alongside movement.[4]

Guidelines from NICE and OARSI state that therapeutic exercise and education are core treatments for osteoarthritis.[2][3] Pain may rise a little when you start, but consistent practice usually eases symptoms and improves daily function over weeks to months. Arthritis Society Canada echoes this: stay active, strengthen supporting muscles, and pace yourself.[4]

What the research says

  • A 2024 Cochrane review of exercise for knee osteoarthritis (139 trials, more than 12,000 people) found low- to moderate-certainty evidence that land-based exercise improves pain, physical function and quality of life in the short term compared with usual care or limited education. Benefits are modest on average but meaningful for many people.[1]
  • OARSI guidelines list structured land-based exercise (strength, aerobic, balance, or mind-body options such as tai chi or yoga) plus education as core care for knee and hip osteoarthritis.[3]
  • NICE recommends therapeutic exercise tailored to the person, combining local muscle strengthening with general aerobic fitness, and notes that joint pain may increase at the start but regular exercise helps in the long term.[2][6]
  • The GLA:D program, which pairs education with supervised neuromuscular exercise over about six to eight weeks, has been delivered to thousands of people with knee or hip osteoarthritis in Canada, Denmark and Australia, with registry data showing reduced pain and better function and quality of life after the program.[5]
  • Virtual physiotherapy also has supporting evidence: remote, exercise-based care can improve pain and function for knee osteoarthritis, which makes a video visit a practical option when wait times, winter travel or distance get in the way.

How does physiotherapy help arthritis?

A physiotherapist assesses how you move, which muscles are weak or stiff, and which daily tasks bother you. The plan usually includes:

  • Strengthening the muscles that support the joint (for example the quadriceps and glutes for the knee) so the joint takes less load.
  • Aerobic activity at a level you can sustain, such as walking, cycling, swimming or water exercise.
  • Education on pacing, flare management, and why appropriate load is protective, not harmful.
  • Simple habits for stairs, sitting and sleep that reduce unnecessary strain.

Hands-on techniques or heat and ice may be used as extras, but guidelines put exercise and education first.[2][3] For inflammatory arthritis, the program is adjusted around flares: gentler range of motion during a flare, progressive strengthening between them.

Try this today

These are safe starting points for most people with stable osteoarthritis. Stop if pain rises sharply, you feel unstable, or symptoms do not settle within a day or two. A physiotherapist can refine them for you.

1. Sit-to-stand from a chair

Sit in a sturdy chair with feet flat. Lean slightly forward, push through your feet and stand. Slowly sit back down with control. Do 5 to 8 repetitions, then rest. Stop if the joint feels unstable or pain goes beyond a mild increase.

2. Straight-leg raise

Lie on your back or sit. Tighten the thigh of the affected leg and lift the heel a few inches off the surface, keeping the knee straight. Hold 3 to 5 seconds and lower slowly. Do 8 to 10 repetitions. Stop if sharp pain or swelling appears.

3. Short walk with pacing

Walk for 5 to 10 minutes at a conversational pace on level ground or indoors. Note how the joint feels during the walk and the next morning. If it settles, add a minute or two next time. Use walking poles if balance or load is a concern.

4. Heat for morning stiffness

Apply a warm (not hot) pack or take a warm shower for 10 to 15 minutes before moving. Follow with a few of the movements above. Use ice after activity if the joint feels swollen and warm.

Physio tip: A small, short-lived rise in pain during or after exercise is usually fine. If it has settled by the next morning, you are loading the joint at about the right level.

When to see a physio

See a registered physiotherapist if pain is limiting stairs, walking, sleep or work; if stiffness lasts more than 30 minutes most mornings; or if you are unsure how to start exercising without a flare. A physiotherapist can assess you by video, build a plan you can do at home, and adjust it as you progress.

Seek same-day medical care, and do not wait for a routine physio slot, if you have:
  • A joint that is hot and very swollen, especially with a fever.
  • Sudden inability to bear weight on the leg.
  • New numbness or weakness, or changes in bladder or bowel control.
  • Pain after a fall or significant injury.
In an emergency, call 911.

Frequently asked questions

Does physiotherapy cure arthritis?

No. It does not reverse joint changes. Research shows it often reduces pain and improves function so you can stay active. [1][2]

Will exercise wear out my arthritic joint?

Guidelines and reviews indicate the opposite: appropriate strengthening and aerobic exercise are beneficial and do not speed up joint damage when progressed sensibly. [2][3]

Is virtual physiotherapy effective for arthritis?

Evidence supports remote physiotherapy for knee osteoarthritis and other joint pain, with outcomes often similar to in-person care when the focus is exercise and education.

How soon might I notice a difference?

Many people feel changes in strength or daily ease within a few weeks of consistent practice. Larger gains often appear over 6 to 12 weeks. [6]

Does OHIP cover physiotherapy for arthritis?

Public coverage varies by province and is limited. Most people use extended health benefits for physiotherapy, including virtual visits.

Can I do this if I also have rheumatoid arthritis?

Yes, with adjustments. Coordinate with your rheumatologist; a physiotherapist can tailor intensity around flares.
