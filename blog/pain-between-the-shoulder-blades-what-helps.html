Pain Between the Shoulder Blades: What Helps

By the Calm Joints physio team · Updated November 24, 2026 · 7 min read

Pain between the shoulder blades is usually caused by strained or tired muscles and joints in the upper back or neck, often after long periods of sitting, phone use, or repetitive arm work.[1] Regular movement, position changes, and simple shoulder-blade strengthening can ease discomfort and improve how the area works.[2][6] Sudden severe pain with chest symptoms needs urgent medical attention.

Why does the area between my shoulder blades hurt?

The space between your shoulder blades sits over the upper and mid thoracic spine. The main structures that can hurt here are the rhomboid and middle trapezius muscles that hold the shoulder blades back and down, the small facet joints of the thoracic spine, the rib joints, and nerves that refer pain from the neck.

Most of the time the pain is mechanical. It changes with how you sit, move your arms, or turn your head. Upper back pain most commonly comes from soft-tissue strain, repetitive work and sustained loading rather than a single serious disease.[1]

Sitting for hours with the head forward and shoulders rounded keeps the muscles between the blades under constant low load. They fatigue, develop tender spots, and ache. Lifting something heavier than usual, painting a ceiling, or starting a new sport can do the same in a shorter time. Stress can tighten the same muscles further.

Sometimes the source is a bit higher. Irritated joints or discs in the lower neck can refer pain straight into the area between the blades even when the neck itself does not hurt much. Less often, the dorsal scapular nerve that supplies the rhomboids is involved: one study found signs of it in just over half of people with one-sided pain between the shoulder blades.[3]

In a smaller number of cases the pain is referred from somewhere else, such as the heart, esophagus, gallbladder or lungs. That is why sudden severe or tearing pain, especially with chest pressure, shortness of breath or sweating, is never something to watch at home.

Is it always “bad posture”?

No. There is no single perfect posture. Bodies are built to move and change position. What tends to bother the area is staying in one shape for a long time, especially a rounded, head-forward one, without giving the muscles a chance to unload.

Age-related changes in the thoracic joints can add stiffness. Prolonged sitting, reduced movement and weaker supporting muscles are common contributors to back pain in general, and regular movement is one of the most useful things you can do.[4]

If the pain only appears after a specific activity and settles within a day or two, it is often simple overload. If it lingers, wakes you, or spreads into the arm with tingling, a physiotherapist can sort out whether the driver is local muscle, thoracic joints, or something referred from the neck.

What the research says

  • A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis found that exercise programs focused on the shoulder-blade muscles improved shoulder function more than comparison treatments, with clearer benefits when programs lasted six weeks or longer.[2]
  • Reviews of scapular stabilization exercises show modest reductions in pain and improvements in function when added to general care, though the certainty of evidence is low to moderate.[6]
  • Evidence specifically for thoracic spine pain is thinner than for neck or low back pain. The consistent practical message is to keep moving, strengthen the muscles that control the shoulder blades, and avoid long unbroken periods in one position.
  • Canadian research on virtual musculoskeletal care reports positive patient experiences, and assessment and exercise guidance for upper back and shoulder pain translate well to video.[5]

Try this today

These are gentle, commonly used movements. Stop any exercise that makes the pain sharper, spreads, or leaves you worse the next day. If you have known neck or shoulder problems, start even smaller.

1. Seated shoulder-blade squeezes

Sit tall with feet on the floor. Gently draw the shoulder blades back and slightly down as if holding a pencil between them. Hold 5 seconds, release. Repeat 8 to 10 times, a few times a day. Stop if you feel pinching in the front of the shoulder.

2. Chair thoracic extension

Sit on a sturdy chair with hands behind your head or across your chest. Lean the upper back gently over the top of the chair back so the mid-back extends a little. Hold 5 to 10 seconds, return. Repeat 5 to 8 times. Keep the movement small and stop if you feel sharp pain or dizziness.

3. Frequent position changes

Every 25 to 30 minutes, stand, roll the shoulders slowly backward five times, and take a short walk if you can. The goal is not perfect posture; it is not staying still.

4. Short, easy walks

A 10-minute walk at a comfortable pace often loosens the upper back. Swing the arms naturally. If walking increases the pain, shorten it or try the seated movements first.

Physio tip: Heat for 10 to 15 minutes can feel good on tight muscles, and ice can help after a new activity. Neither replaces movement.

When to see a physio

See a registered physiotherapist if the pain has lasted more than a couple of weeks, keeps returning, affects your sleep or work, or you are unsure how to start moving again without making it worse. A virtual visit lets you show how you sit and move, get a clear plan, and progress the exercises safely from home.

Seek same-day medical care, and do not wait for a routine physio slot, if you have:
  • Sudden severe or tearing pain between the shoulder blades, especially with chest pressure, shortness of breath, sweating or collapse (call 911).
  • Fever, unexplained weight loss, or pain that is constant and worse at night.
  • New weakness, numbness or tingling that spreads into an arm or leg.
  • Difficulty controlling your bladder or bowel.
  • Pain after a significant fall or accident.
In an emergency, call 911.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common cause of pain between the shoulder blades?

Muscle fatigue and strain in the rhomboids and middle trapezius from prolonged sitting or repetitive arm use is the most frequent cause. Joint irritation in the thoracic spine or referred pain from the neck can produce the same ache. [1]

Will stretching make it better?

Gentle movement and light strengthening usually help more than aggressive stretching. Over-stretching a tired muscle can sometimes irritate it further. Start small and build. [2]

How long does it usually last?

Many cases of simple muscle overload settle within a few days to a couple of weeks once you change positions more often and add light exercise. Persistent pain benefits from a proper assessment.

Can virtual physiotherapy help with this?

Yes. Assessment of upper back and shoulder-blade pain focuses on posture, movement and exercise, all of which can be done clearly on video. [5]

Is this related to a heart problem?

Most of the time it is not. However, any sudden severe pain between the shoulder blades with chest symptoms, shortness of breath or sweating should be treated as a medical emergency until proven otherwise. [1]
