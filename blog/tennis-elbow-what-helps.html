Tennis Elbow: What Helps

By the Calm Joints physio team · Updated November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

Tennis elbow is pain on the outside of the elbow from overload of the forearm tendons. Most people improve within a year with load management and gradual strengthening.[3] A registered physiotherapist can help you match the right amount of load so daily tasks feel easier sooner.[1][2]

What is tennis elbow?

Tennis elbow, also called lateral epicondylalgia or lateral epicondylitis, is pain and tenderness where the tendons of the forearm muscles attach to the bony bump on the outside of the elbow. It is an overload problem, not usually a true inflammatory “itis.” Repetitive gripping, twisting or lifting can irritate the tendon. You do not need to play tennis to get it. Office work, gardening, carrying bags or using tools can all contribute.[5][6]

The pain often starts gradually. It can hurt when you grip a mug, open a jar or shake hands. Some people notice it more in the dominant arm.

How long does tennis elbow last?

Most cases improve over time. Research on people who received little or no specific treatment found that about 90% reported improvement by one year.[3] Symptoms often ease in a stepwise way, and a smaller number have longer-lasting symptoms.

Physiotherapy that includes progressive exercise can reduce pain and disability in the short term compared with waiting alone. Long-term differences are smaller because so many people improve either way. Corticosteroid injections can give quicker short-term relief but are linked with higher recurrence later, so they are not first-line for most people.[1]

What the research says

  • A large randomised trial compared physiotherapy (mobilisation with movement plus exercise), corticosteroid injection and a wait-and-see approach. At six weeks, physiotherapy was better than waiting. At one year most people in the physiotherapy and wait-and-see groups had improved, while the injection group had more recurrence.[1]
  • A 2024 Cochrane review of manual therapy and exercise for lateral elbow pain found low-certainty evidence that these approaches may slightly reduce pain and disability at the end of treatment compared with minimal care. Benefits were not always large or sustained.[2]
  • Canadian shoulder and elbow specialists note that outcomes can be similar between no active treatment, physiotherapy and injections in the short to medium term, so progressive loading under guidance is reasonable for many people.[4]
  • Reviews of telehealth for musculoskeletal pain show that video-based and app-supported rehabilitation can reduce pain and improve function compared with usual care.[7]

Try this today

These are gentle starting points. Stop any movement that causes sharp pain, numbness, or swelling that worsens. If pain stays above a mild level (around 3 out of 10) or does not settle within a day, pause and get advice.

1. Modify the load

Notice which grips or tasks flare the elbow (heavy bags, repeated mouse use, opening jars). Reduce how often or how hard for a few days while keeping the arm moving in other ways. Complete rest is rarely needed.

2. Isometric wrist extension hold

Sit with your forearm supported on a table, palm down, hand over the edge. Use your other hand to gently resist while you try to lift the hand up. Hold 5 to 10 seconds, 5 to 8 times, once or twice a day. The effort should feel mild.

3. Eccentric wrist lowering

Start with the hand raised (wrist extended). Slowly lower the hand over 3 to 5 seconds using the sore arm, then use the other hand to help lift it back up. Do 8 to 12 slow repetitions once a day. Progress only if it stays comfortable.

4. Forearm stretch

Straighten the arm, palm down, and gently pull the fingers back toward you with the other hand until you feel a mild stretch on top of the forearm. Hold 20 to 30 seconds, 2 to 3 times. Do not force it.

Physio tip: A counterforce strap can reduce pain during aggravating tasks by spreading load. Treat it as a helper while you build strength, not a cure.

When to see a physio

See a registered physiotherapist if the pain has lasted more than a few weeks, limits gripping or daily tasks, or keeps returning when you resume activity. A physio can check that the pain is coming from the elbow tendons, rule out other causes, and build a progressive plan that matches your work and hobbies.

Seek same-day medical care, and do not wait for a routine physio slot, if you have:
  • Sudden severe swelling or deformity after an injury.
  • Fever with a hot, red joint.
  • Numbness or weakness that spreads into the hand and does not settle.
  • Pain that wakes you at night and does not ease with position changes.
In an emergency, call 911.

Frequently asked questions

Is tennis elbow the same as golfer’s elbow?

No. Tennis elbow is on the outside of the elbow. Golfer’s elbow is on the inside. Both are overload tendon problems but involve different muscle groups. [5]

Will a brace help?

A counterforce brace or strap can reduce pain during aggravating tasks for some people by spreading load. It is an adjunct, not a cure. [6]

Should I stop all activity?

No. Relative rest from the worst triggers plus gradual loading usually works better than complete immobilisation. Keep moving the shoulder and the rest of the arm. [4]

How soon can I return to sport or work?

It depends on the load required. Many people resume modified activity within weeks once pain is controlled and strength is improving. A physio can help set realistic targets. [4]

Does virtual physio work for elbow pain?

Research on telehealth for musculoskeletal conditions shows that video visits can reduce pain and improve function. Grip, wrist strength and daily tasks can be assessed effectively by video. [7]

When is imaging needed?

Most cases do not need an X-ray or ultrasound at the start. Imaging is more useful if symptoms do not improve as expected or another diagnosis is suspected. [4]
