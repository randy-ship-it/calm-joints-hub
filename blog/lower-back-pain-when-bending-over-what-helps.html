Lower Back Pain When Bending Over: What Helps

By the Calm Joints physio team · Updated November 27, 2026 · 7 min read

Lower back pain when bending over usually comes from how the spine and the muscles around it handle the load of bending forward. Most cases improve with staying active, targeted exercise and better movement habits rather than rest alone.[1][2] Research consistently shows that exercise programmes reduce pain and improve function for people with ongoing low back pain.[3]

Why does my lower back hurt when I bend over?

Bending forward loads the front of the discs and stretches the muscles along the spine. A sudden increase in activity, repeated bending at work or at home, or simply sitting for long periods can leave the back sensitive. Muscle strain, ligament irritation or an irritated disc are common reasons. In many people the pain is non-specific, meaning no single structure is clearly the cause.[1]

Osteoarthritis in the small facet joints or age-related disc changes can also make bending feel stiff or sharp. Arthritis Society Canada notes that exercise to improve spinal mechanics, core strength, and flexibility in the hamstrings and lower back is a key part of managing this kind of pain.[4]

What the research says

  • The NICE guideline on low back pain and sciatica recommends considering a group exercise programme (biomechanical, aerobic, mind-body or a combination) and encouraging people to continue normal activities. It advises against belts, corsets and traction.[1]
  • The 2021 JOSPT clinical practice guideline says physical therapists should use exercise training for chronic low back pain, including trunk strengthening and endurance, specific trunk muscle activation, aerobic exercise and general exercise. For acute pain, exercise including trunk activation can be used.[2]
  • A Cochrane review of exercise for chronic low back pain found moderate-certainty evidence that exercise probably reduces pain by about 15 points on a 0 to 100 scale compared with no treatment, usual care or placebo. Function also improves, though the effect is smaller.[3]
  • Together these support a practical approach: keep moving, strengthen the muscles that support the spine, and avoid prolonged rest.

Try this today

These are gentle options many people find helpful. Stop any movement that increases sharp pain or sends pain down the leg.

1. Pelvic tilt

Lie on your back with knees bent and feet flat. Gently flatten your lower back against the floor by tilting your pelvis, then release. Move slowly through 8 to 10 repetitions. This helps you find a comfortable mid-range position.

2. Cat-cow

On hands and knees, slowly round your back toward the ceiling, then gently arch it while looking slightly forward. Keep the movement small and controlled. Repeat 8 to 10 times. It encourages mobility without forcing end-range bending.

3. Supported hip hinge practice

Stand with feet hip-width apart and a slight bend in the knees. Push your hips back as if closing a car door with your bottom, keeping your spine long rather than rounded. Only go as far as is comfortable, then return. Do 6 to 8 slow repetitions. Practising this pattern can make everyday bending feel more controlled.

4. Short walks

Take a 5 to 10 minute walk at a comfortable pace. Changing position and light aerobic activity often eases stiffness after sitting or bending.

Physio tip: When you need to pick something up, bring it close, bend at the hips and knees, and breathe out as you stand. Avoiding bending altogether tends to make it feel scarier, not safer.

When to see a physio

See a registered physiotherapist if bending pain has lasted more than a couple of weeks, keeps coming back, or is limiting work, sleep or daily tasks. A physiotherapist can assess how you move, guide progressive exercise, and help you get back to the activities that matter to you.

Seek same-day medical care, and do not wait for a routine physio slot, if you have:
  • New difficulty starting to pee, or reduced sensation of flow.
  • New numbness around the genitals or bottom.
  • Loss of control of your bladder or bowel.
  • Severe or worsening weakness in both legs, or sudden pain in both legs.
  • Back pain with fever, unexplained weight loss, or after a significant fall or accident.
In an emergency, call 911.

Frequently asked questions

Is it normal for my lower back to hurt when I bend over?

Yes. It is a common complaint. Most episodes settle with activity and simple exercise rather than complete rest. [1]

Should I rest completely if bending hurts?

No. Prolonged bed rest is not recommended. Gentle movement and short walks are usually better once the worst pain eases. [1]

Will strengthening help my back when I bend?

Research supports trunk strengthening, endurance work and general exercise for reducing pain and improving function in chronic low back pain. [2][3]

When should I worry about pain that goes down my leg?

If pain, numbness or weakness travels below the knee and is new or worsening, or if you have any of the red-flag symptoms above, get assessed promptly. [5]

Does virtual physio work for this?

Yes. Assessment, exercise progression and return-to-activity plans for low back pain translate well to video, so you can work on them from home.
