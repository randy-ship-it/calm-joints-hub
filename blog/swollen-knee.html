Swollen knee: what helps

By the Calm Joints physio team · Updated October 30, 2026 · 8 min read

A swollen knee means extra fluid has collected in the joint or in the soft tissue around it. Without a twist, fall, or blow, the usual reasons are osteoarthritis, a flare of inflammation, a swollen bursa, or a pocket of fluid at the back of the knee, and less often an infection or a crystal attack such as gout.[2][4] Relative rest, a wrapped ice pack, and the leg raised above the heart are the first steps health bodies describe.[1][3] Heat on a knee that is already puffy tends to pull in more fluid, so save heat for stiffness once the swelling has settled.[5]

Why is my knee swollen if I did not injure it?

Mayo Clinic describes a swollen knee as excess fluid in or around the joint, sometimes called an effusion. Trauma is one route. So are overuse and diseases that irritate the lining of the joint, including osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and infection.[2] A StatPearls review of knee effusion lists the same broad groups: injury, inflammatory arthritis, infection, and degenerative change such as osteoarthritis.[4]

The NHS groups the pattern by how the knee looks and feels. Pain and stiffness in both knees, with mild swelling, is more common in older adults and fits osteoarthritis. A knee that is warm and red, and worse with kneeling, can be bursitis. A knee that is hot and red with a sudden attack of severe pain can be gout or septic arthritis. Those last two are not a home-care problem.[1]

Fluid can also sit behind the knee in a Baker’s cyst, which often tags along with an effusion already in the joint. The front of the knee can look only a little bigger while the back feels tight when you bend.

A puffier knee after a long day on your feet, a new walking route, or a weekend of kneeling is a common story. It is still worth comparing the two knees in a mirror. A joint that is clearly larger than the other one, and that will not fully bend, is carrying more fluid than a bit of end-of-day ache.

Is swelling the same as osteoarthritis?

Often related, not the same thing. Osteoarthritis can irritate the joint lining and leave a modest effusion. EULAR guidance on knee osteoarthritis lists a modest effusion as a possible exam finding, alongside crepitus, bony enlargement, and pain or stiffness with movement. A large, hot, red effusion is a red flag in that same guidance, pointing toward infection, crystals, or other serious bone or joint disease, not a routine flare.[8]

NICE treats typical knee osteoarthritis as a clinical diagnosis in adults 45 and over when pain is related to activity, morning stiffness lasts about 30 minutes or less, and there is no other explanation. Imaging is not required to start care.[6] Swelling that is new, one-sided, and out of proportion to your usual ache is a reason to be seen, even if you already know you have osteoarthritis.

If the main complaint is the first steps of the day rather than a puffy joint, stiff knees in the morning is the closer match. If the knee complains most on the way down, knee pain going down stairs covers that pattern.

What should I do in the first day or two?

For ordinary knee pain and swelling, the NHS suggests putting as little weight on the knee as you can, using an ice pack or a bag of frozen peas wrapped in a tea towel for up to 20 minutes every 2 to 3 hours, and using a pain reliever such as paracetamol or ibuprofen gel or tablets if they are suitable for you.[1] Mayo Clinic’s self-care list is similar: avoid weight-bearing as much as you can, ice for 15 to 20 minutes every 2 to 4 hours with the knee raised above the heart, and a gentle elastic wrap if compression is comfortable.[3]

Arthritis Society Canada is specific about temperature. Cold is the better match for swelling, because it eases inflammation and slows fluid shift into the area. Heat widens blood vessels and can let more fluid in, so it should not go on a joint that is already swollen. Their physiotherapist guidance is a wrapped ice pack for about 10 to 15 minutes, stopped if the skin goes numb.[5] A thin towel between ice and skin is the rule on both the NHS and Arthritis Society pages.

A few practical limits:

  • Do not put ice on skin, and do not sleep with an ice pack on.
  • A compression sleeve should feel snug, not tingly, and not leave deep marks. It does not brace the knee against a new injury.
  • Total bed rest for days makes the thigh weaker. Short, gentle bends are better than a locked knee, as long as pain is not rising.
  • Skip anti-inflammatory tablets if you have been told to avoid them. Kidney disease, stomach ulcers, and some heart conditions change that choice. Ask a pharmacist or your prescriber.

In Canada, a virtual physio visit can sort the pattern, the load, and a short exercise plan without a waiting room. Many workplace extended health plans cover physiotherapy. Public coverage is narrower and depends on the province, age, and whether you have just left hospital. Check your plan before you assume a visit is out of pocket.

If the knee is hot, you feel feverish, or you cannot put weight through the leg, skip the ice routine and use the urgent list below.

What the research says

Fluid on the knee is a sign, not a diagnosis. The StatPearls review treats effusion as fluid from several different processes, and it treats a hot, systemically unwell joint as infection until proven otherwise.[4] That is why a single swollen knee with fever is a same-day medical problem, not a stretch problem.

For the osteoarthritis pattern, the treatments with the strongest guideline support are education and exercise, plus weight management when it applies. OARSI lists structured land-based exercise, with or without dietary weight management, as a core treatment for knee osteoarthritis, and strongly recommends topical non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.[7] NICE tells clinicians to offer therapeutic exercise tailored to the person, local strengthening and general fitness, and to warn that pain can rise a little when exercise starts. Sticking with it is what reduces pain and improves function. NICE also notes that losing about 10 percent of body weight is likely to help more than 5 percent, and that any loss can help.[6]

A Cochrane review of land-based exercise for knee osteoarthritis found that exercise reduces pain and improves function compared with non-exercise control, with few adverse events reported.[9] That does not mean a puffy, hot knee should be loaded hard on day one. It means that once infection and a severe flare are off the table, movement is part of care, not the thing to avoid forever.

Telehealth physiotherapy is a reasonable way to get that plan. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis in Annals of Physical and Rehabilitation Medicine found telerehabilitation favourable to control treatments across musculoskeletal outcomes, with effects in osteoarthritis similar to the comparison treatments, and concluded it should be considered when an ideal in-person programme is not available.[10] A video visit does not replace urgent care for a red, hot knee.

Try this today

Stop any of these if pain jumps, the knee feels unstable, or swelling is clearly increasing while you do them. They are not for a knee that is hot, red, and paired with fever.

1. Leg up, ankle pumps

Lie on a sofa or bed. Stack pillows so the swollen knee is above the level of your heart. Point and flex the foot 15 times, slowly. Rest. Repeat once more. Stop if the calf is tender, hot, or newly swollen on its own.

2. Wrapped ice, timed

Wrap a cold pack or frozen peas in a tea towel. Rest it on the puffy part of the knee for 10 to 15 minutes, and not more than 20. Repeat every 2 to 3 hours while you are awake on the first day. Stop if the skin hurts, turns very red, or goes numb. Do not use heat on this knee today.

3. Heel slides, once the ice is off

Lie or sit with the leg supported. Slowly slide the heel toward your buttock until you feel a mild stretch, not a sharp pinch. Hold 2 or 3 seconds. Slide back out. Do 8 to 10 bends, once or twice today. Stop if the knee locks or the swelling surges in the hour after.

4. Sit-to-stand practice, only if you can weight-bear

If you can walk to the kitchen without the knee giving way, practise 5 slow sit-to-stands from a firm chair, using your hands on the armrests. Keep the knees tracking over the toes. Skip it if you cannot trust the leg, or if pain is more than mild during the stand.

Physio tip: None of these replaces a program matched to your knee. They are safe starters a physio can progress once they have seen how the knee moves.

When to see a physio

Book a visit if the knee has been puffy for more than a few days, if stairs or a short walk have got harder, or if you keep swelling after ordinary days. A registered physiotherapist can check how you bend, how you stand, and which loads to change, then give you a short plan you can do at home.

Calm Joints visits are virtual, with registered physiotherapists, for people across Canada. Book a virtual visit at calmjoints.org.

Get urgent care, and do not wait for a physio appointment, if:[1][11]
  • The knee is hot or red and you have a fever, chills, or feel suddenly unwell. The NHS lists a high temperature with redness or heat around the knee as a reason to get advice now.
  • You cannot move the knee or put any weight on it, the knee has changed shape, or it locks or gives way.
  • One calf is newly swollen, warm, or tender, especially after a flight, surgery, or a period of sitting still. If it comes with chest pain or breathlessness, call 911.
  • Swelling arrived with a blow, a twist, or a pop and has not settled.
Same-week care is sensible if swelling is still obvious after a few days of ice and relative rest, if the knee is giving way, or if you have gout, rheumatoid arthritis, or a recent joint replacement and this flare feels different. In an emergency, call 911.

Frequently asked questions

Can a knee swell with no injury?

Yes. Mayo Clinic and StatPearls both list osteoarthritis, inflammatory arthritis, infection, and overuse alongside trauma. A mild puffiness after a busier day is common. A single hot, red, very painful knee is not something to explain away as wear and tear. [2][4]

Should I use ice or heat on a swollen knee?

Ice, with a cloth between the pack and the skin. Arthritis Society Canada advises against heat on a swollen joint because heat can increase fluid in the area. Heat is more useful later, for stiffness, once the puffiness has gone down. [5]

How long does fluid on the knee last?

It depends on the cause. Simple swelling after extra load often eases over a few days with relative rest, ice, and elevation. The NHS suggests seeing a GP if knee pain has not improved within a few weeks, and getting urgent advice if the knee is badly swollen, has changed shape, or comes with fever. [1]

Is walking good when the knee is swollen?

Short, easy walking is fine if you can weight-bear and pain stays mild. Long standing and hills can feed the swelling in the first day or two. NICE and OARSI still put exercise at the centre of osteoarthritis care once a flare is settling. [6][7]

Will a virtual physio visit help a swollen knee?

It can help with the load, the swelling routine, and a strengthening plan you can do at home. It cannot rule out infection or drain a knee. Use urgent care for a hot joint with fever, a knee you cannot stand on, or a new one-sided calf swelling. A 2024 review supports telerehabilitation when in-person care is hard to reach, with effects in osteoarthritis similar to comparison treatments. [10]
