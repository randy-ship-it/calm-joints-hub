Neck pain from your phone: what helps

By the Calm Joints physio team · Updated November 3, 2026 · 8 min read

Looking down at a phone increases the load on your neck and is linked to more neck pain in adults who use devices heavily.[1] Research shows that adults with neck pain often have a more forward head position than people without pain, and that targeted exercises for the deep neck muscles and posture can reduce pain and improve how the neck sits.[2][3][4] Most cases are mechanical and improve with movement, position changes, and time. This is educational information, not personal medical advice.

Why does my neck hurt after using my phone?

Your head weighs roughly 4 to 5 kg when it sits balanced over your shoulders. Tilting it forward to look at a screen shifts that weight farther from the base of the neck. The muscles at the back of the neck and upper back work harder to hold the position. Over hours of scrolling, those muscles fatigue, the joints stiffen, and you feel ache, tightness, or a headache that starts at the base of the skull.

A 2025 meta-analysis of smartphone overuse found that people who overused their phones had more than twice the odds of neck pain compared with those who did not (pooled adjusted odds ratio 2.34).[1] Cross-sectional studies also show that adults with neck pain tend to have a smaller craniovertebral angle—a measure of how far the head sits forward—than people without pain. One systematic review reported a mean difference of about 2.9 degrees.[2]

The pattern is common in people who spend long stretches looking down, whether on a phone, tablet, or laptop. It is not usually a sign that the neck is “worn out” or that discs are collapsing. Guidelines treat most of this as non-specific neck pain: pain and stiffness without clear nerve findings or serious disease.[5][6]

Is “text neck” a real diagnosis?

The term is popular, but it is not a formal medical diagnosis. It describes the posture and the symptoms that follow prolonged looking down. A longitudinal study found that “text neck” posture alone did not predict new neck pain over a year; low activity and poor sleep were stronger predictors. Still, the mechanical load is real, and people who already have neck pain often report that phone use makes it worse.[1][2]

Canadian physiotherapists see the same pattern in desk workers and people who use phones for work or personal time. Extended health benefits in most provinces cover virtual physiotherapy, so assessment and a tailored plan are available without leaving home.

If the neck is stiffest first thing in the morning rather than after a scrolling session, neck pain in the morning is the closer match. Desk posture that also loads the low back is covered in lower back pain from sitting.

What does the research say about exercises?

Systematic reviews support exercise for people with forward head posture and neck pain. A 2026 meta-analysis of therapeutic exercise found that programs focused on the neck, or the neck plus the upper back, improved the craniovertebral angle and reduced disability compared with control conditions. Combined programs also lowered pain intensity.[3] Another review of deep cervical flexor training (the small muscles that nod the head) showed benefits for neuromuscular control and posture.[4]

The changes are modest and take weeks of consistent practice. Exercise is not a quick fix, and evidence quality is often low to moderate. The practical message is that strengthening the muscles that hold the head in a better position, plus reducing the hours spent looking down, helps most people more than passive treatments alone.

NICE Clinical Knowledge Summaries recommend staying active, avoiding collars, and considering physiotherapy for non-specific neck pain that does not settle.[5] NHS pages give the same advice: gentle movement, heat or cold for comfort, and seeking help if symptoms persist or include red-flag signs.[6]

Try this today

These are gentle starting points. Stop if pain increases or spreads into the arm, or if you feel dizzy, numb, or weak. They are not a substitute for assessment.

1. Chin tuck (deep neck flexor activation)

Sit or stand tall. Gently draw your chin straight back, as if making a double chin, without tilting the head down. Hold 5 seconds, relax, repeat 8 to 10 times. You should feel light work under the front of the jaw and upper neck. Stop if it pinches or causes headache.

2. Shoulder-blade squeeze

Sit tall. Gently draw the shoulder blades down and together, as if sliding them into back pockets. Hold 5 seconds, release. Do 8 to 10. Keep the neck relaxed. This supports the upper back so the neck does not work alone.

3. Phone position reset

Hold the phone higher, closer to eye level, for short periods. Set a timer for every 20 to 30 minutes of use and look up, stand, or do one chin tuck. Even brief changes reduce the total load.

4. Heat or movement for stiffness

A warm shower or heat pack on the upper back and neck for 10 to 15 minutes can ease morning or end-of-day stiffness. Then do the chin tuck and shoulder-blade moves slowly.

Physio tip: None of these replaces a program matched to your neck. They are safe starters a physio can progress once they have seen how you hold devices and how the neck moves.

When to see a physio

Book a visit if neck pain has lasted more than a few weeks, limits your work or sleep, or keeps returning with phone or computer use. A registered physiotherapist can check movement, strength, and posture, then build a plan that fits your day. Virtual care works well for this: you show how you hold your phone or sit at a desk, and you get exercises and load advice you can do at home. Extended health benefits usually cover it.

Get urgent care, and do not wait for a physio appointment, if:[5][6]
  • Sudden severe pain after trauma, or pain that is constant and unrelenting.
  • Weakness or numbness in an arm or both arms, or difficulty walking.
  • Bowel or bladder changes with neck pain.
  • Fever with neck stiffness, or you feel suddenly unwell.
Same-week care is sensible if neck pain keeps returning with phone or desk work after a few weeks of position changes and gentle exercise. In an emergency, call 911.

Frequently asked questions

Does looking at my phone damage my neck permanently?

No. The increased load is temporary. Most people improve with less time in the flexed position and simple strengthening. Serious structural change from phone use alone is rare.

How long until exercises help?

Many people notice less stiffness in 1 to 2 weeks of consistent practice. Larger improvements in pain and posture usually take 4 to 8 weeks. Consistency matters more than intensity. [3]

Should I use a neck brace or collar?

Guidelines advise against routine collars for non-specific neck pain. They limit movement and can slow recovery. Use one only if a clinician specifically recommends it for a short period. [5]

Can virtual physiotherapy help with phone-related neck pain?

Yes. A physio can watch how you hold devices, check relevant strength and mobility, and give a progressive plan. Many Canadian extended health plans cover virtual visits.

When is neck pain not just from my phone?

If pain radiates into the arm with weakness or numbness, follows a fall or accident, comes with fever, or is getting steadily worse despite rest and movement, it needs medical assessment. Phone posture may still be a factor, but other causes should be ruled out. [5][6]
