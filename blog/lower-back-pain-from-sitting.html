Lower back pain from sitting: what helps

By the Calm Joints physio team · Updated October 13, 2026 · 9 min read

Sitting can make an already sensitive lower back feel worse, but long hours in a chair are not, on their own, proof that the chair caused the pain. Most lower back pain is non-specific, meaning it is not tied to a single dangerous structure, and guidelines favour staying active over rest. Research suggests exercise eases pain compared with doing nothing, and regular leisure activity is linked with a lower chance of frequent or chronic low back pain.

Why does my lower back hurt when I sit?

The lumbar area is the stretch of spine between the bottom of the ribs and the top of the pelvis. Pain there is common. NICE describes most of it as non-specific: mechanical or musculoskeletal, and not linked to a serious cause in the large majority of people. Sciatica, pain that runs into the leg from a nerve, shows up in a smaller share of cases.[3]

A long sit loads the same tissues in the same shape. Hips stay bent. The back stays quiet. People often slump, then brace, then shift. That pattern can feel like stiffness across the belt line, an ache that builds through the afternoon, or a sharp catch when you stand up. Driving, a home-office chair, and a winter commute that ends in another hour on the couch can stack the same way.

What the research does not support is a simple story that sitting, by itself, wrecks backs. Reviews of occupational sitting find an association with musculoskeletal complaints, and the causal evidence is limited. A clearer signal comes from movement. In a meta-analysis of 36 prospective cohort studies (158,475 people), moderate or high leisure-time physical activity was linked with an 11 to 16 percent lower risk of frequent or chronic low back pain, compared with no regular activity. Short bouts of pain over a month or a year were not clearly reduced. Activity looks protective against the pain that hangs around, not a shield against every ache.[4]

Is my chair the problem?

A better chair can feel kinder. It is rarely the whole fix. NICE advises against belts and corsets for low back pain with or without sciatica.[3] A lumbar roll, a sit-stand desk, or a new cushion can be useful experiments. They are not a treatment plan.

Try this check before you buy anything. Can you get both feet on the floor? Are your hips slightly higher than your knees, or level with them? Can you change position without a struggle? If the answer is yes and the ache still builds, the missing piece is usually movement, not another gadget.

Change position before the ache peaks. A short stand, a walk to the kettle, or a few slow hip hinges often do more than a perfect 90-degree posture you cannot hold. There is no single correct sitting shape. There is a shape you can leave.

What the research says

Four lines of evidence are worth knowing in plain language.

First, exercise helps chronic low back pain. A 2021 Cochrane review (Hayden and colleagues, including work led from Dalhousie University in Halifax) found moderate-certainty evidence that exercise is probably better than no treatment, usual care, or placebo for pain. At the earliest follow-up, the mean difference was about 15 points on a 100-point pain scale (95% CI −18.3 to −12.2), which the reviewers called clinically important. Gains in function were smaller and did not meet their threshold for a clinically important difference. Certainty was limited by bias in many trials, including the difficulty of blinding exercise.[1]

Second, no single exercise brand wins for everyone. A 2020 network meta-analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine looked at specific modes in non-specific chronic low back pain. Pilates, stabilisation and motor-control work, resistance training, and aerobic exercise ranked well, depending on whether the outcome was pain, function, or mental health. Stretching and McKenzie exercise did not clearly beat a true control for pain or function. The evidence was low quality. The practical reading: a program you will actually do beats the theoretically perfect one.[2]

Third, guidelines agree on the direction. NICE NG59 says people with low back pain, with or without sciatica, should get advice tailored to them, including encouragement to continue normal activities. It also says to consider a group exercise program (biomechanical, aerobic, mind-body, or a mix) for a flare. Manual therapy can be considered only as part of a package that includes exercise. Rest is not the plan.[3]

Fourth, virtual physio can be a real option when getting to a clinic is the barrier. A 2024 systematic review of randomized trials in musculoskeletal conditions, including low back pain, found telerehabilitation comparable to clinic-based rehab for pain and function, not superior, with lower cost and high adherence.[6] That matches how many Canadians actually live: a snow day in the GTA, a shift that ends after the clinic closes, or a drive you would rather not make when your back already feels fragile.

None of this is a promise that a particular stretch will fix a particular disc. It is a consistent message: understand the pain, keep moving, and get help if it is not settling or if red flags show up.

Should I rest until it calms down?

A day of lighter loads is reasonable when a flare is sharp. A week on the couch usually is not. NICE's self-management advice centres on information and staying with normal activities.[3] Fear that movement will break something is common, and it is the piece that keeps people stuck. Most non-specific low back pain is safe to move with, inside a range that does not escalate symptoms.

Stop and get urgent, same-day medical care if you notice any of the following:[5]
  • New trouble passing urine, loss of the sensation of urine flow, new incontinence, or numbness around the genitals, anus, or inner thighs (possible cauda equina syndrome).
  • New or progressive weakness in a leg or foot, or a foot that drags.
  • Back pain with fever, or feeling generally unwell.
  • Pain after a significant fall or other trauma.
  • A history of cancer with new spine pain, unexplained weight loss, or pain that is unrelenting at night.
  • Known osteoporosis with new spine pain, or pain in someone on long-term steroids or with a suppressed immune system.
These need same-day medical care, not a blog and not a booking form. If you are unsure, err on the side of urgent assessment. In an emergency, call 911.

Try this today

These are starting points for non-specific aching that is not paired with the red flags above. Stop if pain spreads down a leg, if you feel new numbness or weakness, or if a movement clearly makes the next hour worse. Mild stiffness that eases as you move is acceptable.

1. The 30-minute position change

Set a timer for 30 minutes during desk blocks. When it goes, stand for at least one minute. Walk to a window, roll your shoulders, and sit back down in a slightly different shape. You are not chasing perfect posture. You are interrupting one long hold.

How to do it: feet flat, stand without using your hands if you can, take 10 slow steps, sit again. Stop if standing itself shoots pain down a leg.

2. Hip hinge with a counter

Stand facing a kitchen counter, hands lightly on the edge. Soften your knees. Send your hips back as if closing a car door with your pelvis, until you feel a gentle pull in the back of the thighs or a mild stretch across the low back. Pause. Return by pushing the floor away.

Do 8 slow reps, once or twice a day. Stop if pain sharpens or travels below the knee.

3. Supine knee rock

Lie on your back with knees bent and feet on the floor or bed. Let both knees fall a short way to one side, only as far as the low back stays comfortable, then to the other side. Move slowly. 8 rocks each way.

This is a mobility snack, not a twist contest. Stop if you get dizzy, or if leg symptoms increase.

4. A 10-minute walk

If the sidewalk is clear, walk 10 minutes at a pace where you can talk. Winter ice in Toronto and the rest of Ontario is a real limit: use indoor halls, a mall, or a treadmill if footing is poor. Walking is one of the aerobic options that show up favourably in exercise research for low back pain.[2] Stop if pain is escalating rather than settling as you warm up.

Physio tip: Leave the chair before the ache arrives, not after. A one-minute stand every half hour usually beats a perfect posture you hold until it hurts.

When to see a physio

Book a physio visit if the ache has lasted more than a couple of weeks, if sitting is starting to shrink your day, or if you are unsure what is safe. You do not need to wait until you cannot work. A registered physiotherapist can check red flags, watch how you sit and stand, and build a short program around the exercise types that research supports: control, strength, and easy aerobic work.

Calm Joints visits are virtual, from home, with registered physiotherapists. Many extended health plans cover physiotherapy, including virtual care. Rules differ by plan and province. In Ontario, government-funded community physiotherapy is limited to specific groups and situations, not a general benefit for every adult, so a plan or private visit is the usual route for desk-related back pain.[7]

Frequently asked questions

Does sitting cause lower back pain?

Sitting is associated with more musculoskeletal complaints in some studies, but it has not been shown to be a sole cause. Inactivity is the stronger signal. Leisure-time activity is linked with a lower risk of frequent or chronic low back pain.

How often should I stand up?

There is no magic interval. Changing position about every 30 minutes is a practical place to start, with at least a minute on your feet. The aim is variety, not a rigid posture.

Are core exercises necessary?

Stabilisation and motor-control exercise ranked well for function in a 2020 network meta-analysis, alongside resistance and aerobic work for other outcomes. They are one useful option, not a requirement. A program you can repeat matters more than a perfect exercise name.

Should I use heat or ice?

Either can be a comfort tool for a stiff back. Neither replaces movement. Skip ice or heat on skin that is numb, broken, or already very irritated, and do not use them as a reason to stay still all day.

Can virtual physio help lower back pain?

Research on telerehabilitation for musculoskeletal pain, including low back pain, finds it comparable to clinic-based rehab for pain and function, not better. It is a sound option when travel, weather, or schedule make an in-person visit the barrier.

When is back pain an emergency?

Get same-day care for new bladder or bowel changes, saddle numbness, progressive leg weakness, fever with spine pain, pain after serious trauma, or unrelenting night pain with a history of cancer, osteoporosis, or immune suppression.
