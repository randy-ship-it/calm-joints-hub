Outer Knee Pain: What Helps (IT Band)

By the Calm Joints physio team · Updated November 27, 2026 · 7 min read

Pain on the outside of the knee is often iliotibial band syndrome, a common overuse problem in runners, cyclists and walkers.[2] Structured physiotherapy focused on hip strengthening and adjusting how much you do usually reduces pain and improves function within weeks for most people, and surgery is rarely needed.[1][3][4]

What causes pain on the outside of the knee?

The iliotibial (IT) band is a thick strip of connective tissue that runs down the outside of your thigh from the hip to just below the knee. It helps steady the hip and knee when you walk or run.[1]

Pain usually shows up where the band crosses a bony bump on the outside of the knee, just above the joint line. It often starts during activity and eases with rest. Common triggers include a sudden jump in running or cycling distance, hills, banked surfaces, or not enough recovery. Weakness in the hip and buttock muscles can add to the load on the outside of the knee.[2][5]

Experts now think the problem is more about compression of sensitive tissue under the band than simple friction. Either way, the practical approach is the same: reduce the load that bothers it and build the strength that supports the knee.[2]

Who gets outer knee pain?

It is common in people who run or cycle regularly. Studies report it in roughly 2 to 25% of active people, and it is the second most frequent overuse knee problem after pain around the kneecap.[2] Women who run appear to get it more often than men. It can also show up in hikers, soccer players, or anyone who repeats knee-bending movements for long periods. In Canada, winter sidewalks, indoor track running and spring training ramps can all play a role.

What the research says

  • A 2020 systematic review found that conservative care (rest, physiotherapy, activity changes) effectively reduces symptoms in athletes, with follow-up to six months. Surgery is reserved for the few people who do not improve.[3]
  • A 2024 systematic review of conservative treatment in runners looked at 13 studies (201 people). Hip abductor strengthening was the most common approach. Pain dropped 27 to 100% and function improved 10 to 57% over two to eight weeks, though the studies were too different to combine into one number.[4]
  • The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes that most people recover and return to running within about six weeks of non-surgical care that includes physical therapy focused on stretching and strengthening around the hip and knee.[1]
  • There is no single strong guideline, but the consistent message is that structured rehabilitation, especially hip strength work, is the sensible first step. Most people do not need injections or surgery.

Try this today

These are gentle, commonly used movements, not a full program. Stop any exercise if pain rises above a mild 3 out of 10 or if you feel sharp catching.

1. Side-lying hip lift

Lie on your side with the sore leg on top. Keep the top leg straight and slightly behind the bottom leg. Slowly lift it about 30 to 40 cm, hold one second, and lower with control. Do 8 to 12 reps, 2 sets. Keep your hips stacked and do not roll forward.

2. Clamshell

Lie on your side with knees bent about 45 degrees and feet together. Keep your feet touching and lift the top knee as far as is comfortable without rolling the pelvis. Lower slowly. Do 10 to 15 reps, 2 sets.

3. Short walk on flat ground

If walking feels fine, do 10 to 15 minutes on level ground or a treadmill. Skip hills and banked paths for now. If pain starts, shorten the time.

4. Relative rest from the aggravating activity

Cut the distance or time of the activity that brings on the pain by about half for a week or two, then add it back slowly, no more than about 10% per week once the pain is quiet.

Physio tip: Foam rolling may feel good, but it does not lengthen the IT band. Hip strength and a sensible build-up in distance do more of the work.

When to see a physio

See a registered physiotherapist if the pain has lasted more than a couple of weeks, keeps returning when you try to run or cycle, or is changing how you move. A physiotherapist can check your strength, watch how you move, and build a simple plan you can do at home. A doctor’s referral is usually not required.

Seek same-day medical care, and do not wait for a routine physio slot, if you have:
  • Sudden locking, giving way, or a large swelling after a twist.
  • New knee pain with fever, redness or warmth.
  • Pain at night that does not ease with position changes.
  • Numbness, tingling or weakness in the foot.
  • Being unable to put weight on the leg after an injury.
In an emergency, call 911.

Frequently asked questions

Is outer knee pain always the IT band?

No. Other things, such as a meniscus problem, arthritis or pain referred from the hip, can feel similar. A proper assessment rules those out. [2]

How long does IT band pain usually last?

Many people improve over two to eight weeks with activity changes and hip strengthening. A full return to previous mileage can take longer. [4]

Should I stretch the IT band?

Gentle mobility is fine, but aggressive stretching of the band itself has limited evidence. Strengthening the hip muscles is better supported. [4]

Can I keep walking?

Yes, if walking does not increase the pain. Short, flat walks are usually fine and often helpful.

Do I need a scan?

Usually not. Diagnosis is clinical. Imaging is mainly used to rule out other problems if symptoms do not settle. [2]

Does virtual physio work for this?

Many people with IT band symptoms do well with guided home exercises and load advice, which translate well to a video visit.
