How long does frozen shoulder last?

By the Calm Joints physio team · Updated October 9, 2026 · 7 min read

Often about 1 to 3 years. Frozen shoulder usually moves through three stages: a painful freezing stage, a stiff frozen stage, and a thawing stage when everyday motion slowly comes back. Cleveland Clinic describes symptoms as typically easing within 1 to 3 years, and the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) notes that full recovery may take up to 3 years.[2][3] The NHS puts the same idea in plainer words: the shoulder can stay painful and stiff for months, sometimes years, and it usually does settle.[1]

What people mean by a frozen shoulder

The everyday picture is a shoulder that will not do ordinary jobs. A coat sleeve is a struggle. A mug on the second shelf stays there. Reaching for a seatbelt, washing your hair, or sleeping on that side can all hurt. The pain is often worse at night and can break up sleep.[1]

The medical name is adhesive capsulitis. The shoulder is a ball-and-socket joint. A strong sleeve of connective tissue, the capsule, wraps the joint. In frozen shoulder that capsule thickens, tightens, and gets inflamed. Bands of scar tissue, called adhesions, can form, and there is often less of the fluid that lets the joint glide.[2][3] The NHS describes the same process as tissue around the joint becoming inflamed, then tighter, which is what causes the pain.[1]

Two things make this different from a simple strain. Motion is limited both when you try to lift the arm and when someone else moves it for you. And it does not turn around in a week or two. It develops in stages.[2][3]

It is most common between about 40 and 60, and more often in women, though it can happen to anyone. Diabetes and thyroid conditions raise the risk. So does time in a sling after a fracture, a rotator cuff problem, or surgery, because the shoulder stays still.[1][2][3] Often there is no clear reason at all.[1]

The freezing, frozen, and thawing stages

These ranges come from patient guides, not from a timer on your shoulder. One person's freezing stage can be shorter than another's. The labels are still useful, because what helps in month two is not always what helps in month fourteen.

Freezing

Pain builds, and the shoulder loses range. Reaching, tucking in a shirt, or rolling over in bed gets harder. Night pain is common. AAOS and Cleveland Clinic both put this stage at about 6 weeks to 9 months.[2][3]

Frozen

Pain may ease here, which can feel like progress, but the stiffness stays. Daily tasks are still awkward: a jacket, a high cupboard, fastening something behind your back. AAOS describes this stage as about 4 to 6 months. Cleveland Clinic describes it as about 2 to 6 months.[2][3] Same idea, slightly different window. Expect a few months, not a few days.

Thawing

Motion slowly returns. Strength and reach come back toward normal, or close to it. Both AAOS and Cleveland Clinic put this stage at about 6 months to 2 years.[2][3] It is the longest stretch for many people, and it is easy to think nothing is changing because the gains are small from week to week.

So how long is the whole thing?

Add the stages up and you land near the figure most guides use. Cleveland Clinic says symptoms typically get better within 1 to 3 years, and that full or nearly full recovery is often seen after about 2 years.[3] AAOS says frozen shoulder tends to get better after a period of worsening symptoms, although full recovery may take up to 3 years, and that it generally improves without surgery even though that can take up to 3 years.[2] The NHS says it can take months or years, and that the pain and stiffness usually go away eventually.[1]

A few honest qualifiers belong next to that 1-to-3-year line.

  • Some people regain usable motion sooner. Cleveland Clinic notes that simple care, such as pain relief, shoulder exercises, and a cortisone injection, is often enough to restore motion and function within a year or less. Left alone, the shoulder can still improve, usually on a slower clock.[3]
  • Some stiffness can linger. AAOS says that even after several years, motion does not always return completely.[2]
  • Diabetes changes the odds. Frozen shoulder is much more common in people with diabetes. Cleveland Clinic estimates that 10 to 20 percent of people with diabetes develop it. AAOS notes that the stiffness is often greater and lasts longer before thawing, and that if hemoglobin A1C is high, a plan with your doctor to improve glucose control can help the shoulder along.[2][3] The NHS also flags diabetes and regular diabetes check-ups.[1]
  • It can come back, which is uncommon. AAOS says recurrence is more likely if a factor such as diabetes is still present.[2]

None of this is a prediction for one shoulder. It is the pattern described across large patient guides. A physiotherapist or doctor can tell you whether your timeline still fits that pattern, or whether something else, such as arthritis or a rotator cuff tear, needs a look. An X-ray is sometimes used for that. An MRI is not required to recognize frozen shoulder, though it can help if a cuff tear is the question.[2][3]

What actually helps

The aim while the stages run is to keep pain tolerable and to keep as much motion as the shoulder will give you. Forcing a big stretch because you are tired of waiting often backfires.

Gentle range-of-motion work

This is the core of care. AAOS calls physical therapy aimed at shoulder flexibility the main treatment, with stretching and range-of-motion work done with a therapist or as a home plan.[2] The NHS tells people to move the shoulder, because keeping it still makes the pain worse, and to follow exercises from a GP or physiotherapist. It also says not to invent strenuous exercises. Gym equipment can make the pain worse.[1]

AAOS publishes a few gentle examples and says to ask your clinician how many repeats to do. Two that are easy to picture:

  • Lying arm lift. On your back, use the other arm to lift the sore arm overhead only until you feel a gentle stretch. AAOS suggests holding about 15 seconds, then lowering slowly.[2]
  • Across-the-chest stretch. Gently draw one arm across the chest, just below the chin, only as far as it stays tolerable. AAOS suggests holding about 30 seconds.[2]

Stop if pain spikes, the shoulder feels like it gives way, or you cannot use the arm normally afterward. An ache that settles is different from a flare that is still worse that night.

Heat

The NHS suggests a heat pack, or a hot water bottle, wrapped in a tea towel on the shoulder for up to 20 minutes at a time.[1] AAOS notes that heat is sometimes used to loosen the shoulder before stretching.[2] Cleveland Clinic also lists cold packs, alongside heat, as a simple way to ease pain and swelling.[3] Use a cover, not a bare pack on skin. If the shoulder is hot, red, or you feel unwell, skip home heat and use the urgent-care list below.

Seeing a physiotherapist

A registered physiotherapist checks how far the shoulder moves, on your own and with help, and sets a dose you can repeat. NHS physiotherapy for frozen shoulder can include stretching, strength work, posture advice, and help with pain. A course usually lasts at least 6 weeks, and longer if the shoulder is slow. If you are still in pain when the sessions end, go back rather than deciding it failed.[1]

Pain relief is what makes the movement possible. The NHS suggests trying acetaminophen (called paracetamol in the UK) at regular intervals, up to the dose on the label, and an anti-inflammatory such as ibuprofen only if that suits you. Stop an anti-inflammatory if it is not helping. A doctor may later offer stronger pain relief for a short time, or a steroid injection into the shoulder.[1][2] Ask a pharmacist or doctor before you start a new pain medicine, especially if you have stomach, kidney, or heart problems, or you already take other medicines.

Surgery is the exception. AAOS and Cleveland Clinic describe manipulation under anesthesia, and a keyhole release of the tight capsule, mainly when simpler care has not helped. AAOS says that conversation often happens in the frozen stage. Cleveland Clinic mentions considering procedures if noninvasive care has not helped after about a year. Most people improve without an operation.[2][3]

Physio tip: In the freezing stage, chase a calm shoulder tomorrow, not a bigger stretch today. Warm the joint with a wrapped heat pack, move only to a gentle stretch, and stop while it is still tolerable. If the shoulder is clearly worse that night or the next morning, the last session was too much.

When to see a doctor

Book a routine visit if shoulder pain and stiffness are not going away, or if the pain is bad enough that moving the arm is hard. That is the NHS threshold for frozen shoulder, and it is the right time to have someone check the pattern rather than waiting out a year on your own.[1] Broader NHS advice on shoulder pain says to get help if pain is worse or no better after about 2 weeks, or if moving the arm is very difficult.[4]

See a physiotherapist if reaching is shrinking, sleep is breaking up, or you have started avoiding coats, cupboards, and driving. A video visit works well for coaching a gentle plan and watching how you move. It is the wrong door for the urgent signs below.

Calm Joints visits are virtual, with a registered physiotherapist, from home anywhere in Canada. Many extended health plans cover physiotherapy. This page is general education, not a program written for your shoulder.

Get urgent in-person care, and do not wait for a routine physio visit, if the pain is sudden or very bad, you cannot move your arm, the arm or shoulder has changed shape or is badly swollen, you have pins and needles that do not go away, there is no feeling in the arm or shoulder, the arm or shoulder is hot or cold to the touch, the pain started after a fall or accident, you have severe pain in both shoulders, or you feel feverish or unwell. Those can be signs of a break, a dislocation, or a torn tendon.[4] Call 911 if shoulder or arm pain comes with chest pain that feels tight or squeezing, pain spreading into the neck or jaw, severe trouble breathing, pale or grey skin, or if someone passes out.[5]

Frequently asked questions

How long does frozen shoulder last?

Often about 1 to 3 years from the start of pain to the return of usable motion. That course usually runs through a freezing stage, a frozen stage, and a thawing stage. Some people improve sooner, and some keep a little stiffness after the pain has gone.

What are the freezing, frozen, and thawing stages?

Freezing is the painful stage, when movement shrinks. Published ranges put it at about 6 weeks to 9 months. Frozen is when pain may ease but stiffness stays, often for a few months (about 2 to 6 months in Cleveland Clinic's description, and about 4 to 6 months in AAOS guidance). Thawing is the slow return of motion, often lasting 6 months to 2 years.

Does frozen shoulder go away on its own?

It usually improves without surgery, though it can take a long time. NHS guidance says the pain and stiffness usually go away eventually, over months or years. Gentle movement and physiotherapy are still useful. Keeping the arm completely still tends to make the stiffness worse.

What helps a frozen shoulder day to day?

Gentle range-of-motion work, a heat pack wrapped in a towel for up to 20 minutes, and a plan from a physiotherapist. Pain relief recommended by a doctor or pharmacist can make that movement possible. Skip strenuous exercises you invent yourself.

When should I see a doctor for shoulder pain?

See a doctor if shoulder pain and stiffness are not settling, or if the pain makes it hard to move your arm. Ask for urgent care if the pain is sudden or very bad, you cannot move the arm, the shoulder has changed shape or is badly swollen, you have pins and needles or no feeling, the arm is hot or cold, the pain followed a fall, both shoulders are severely painful, or you feel feverish or unwell.

Can a physiotherapist help frozen shoulder?

Yes. Guidance from the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons treats physiotherapy aimed at shoulder flexibility as the main treatment. A physiotherapist checks how far the joint moves and coaches stretches you can do without forcing them. An NHS course often lasts at least 6 weeks, and you can go back if you still need help.
