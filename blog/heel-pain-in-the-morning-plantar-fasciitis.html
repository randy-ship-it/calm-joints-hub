Heel Pain in the Morning: What Helps

By the Calm Joints physio team · Updated November 24, 2026 · 6 min read

Sharp heel pain with the first steps out of bed is a classic sign of plantar fasciitis. Stretching the plantar fascia and calf, plus progressive heel-raise strengthening every other day, can reduce pain and improve function faster than stretching alone for many people.[1][2] Most cases improve with consistent self-care and guidance from a physiotherapist; surgery is rarely needed.

Why does my heel hurt the moment I step out of bed?

The plantar fascia is a thick band of tissue that runs from your heel bone to the base of your toes. It supports the arch and absorbs shock when you walk. When it is overloaded by a sudden increase in standing or walking, unsupportive shoes, tight calves, or higher body weight, the tissue can become sensitive where it attaches at the heel.[4]

After a night of rest the fascia shortens slightly. The first few steps put a sudden stretch on it, which is why the pain is often sharpest then. As you walk a bit the tissue warms up and the pain usually eases, only to return after you sit for a while or at the end of a long day on your feet.

This pattern is common. It does not mean the tissue is permanently damaged. Most people get better with time and the right loading, not complete rest.

What the research says

  • The 2023 JOSPT clinical practice guideline recommends plantar fascia-specific and calf stretching, along with resistance training for the foot and ankle muscles. It supports night splints for people with consistent first-step pain and taping for short-term relief combined with other care.[1]
  • A 2015 randomised trial compared shoe inserts plus daily stretching with shoe inserts plus high-load strength training (slow single-leg heel raises with a towel under the toes, every second day). At three months the strength group reported substantially better foot function; by one year both groups were similar.[2]
  • A 2021 best-practice guide in the British Journal of Sports Medicine recommends a core package of education, stretching and taping for six weeks, then shockwave therapy and custom orthoses if progress is limited.[3]
  • Canadian sources emphasise activity modification, supportive footwear, and calf and plantar fascia stretches as first steps.[4][5]

Try this today

These are gentle starting points. Stop any movement that makes the pain sharply worse or that does not settle within a few minutes after you finish.

1. Plantar fascia stretch (morning priority)

Sit on the edge of the bed. Cross the sore foot over the opposite knee. Gently pull the toes back toward your shin until you feel a stretch along the arch. Hold 10 to 20 seconds, repeat 5 to 10 times, before your first steps.

2. Standing calf stretch

Face a wall. Step the sore leg back, keep the heel on the floor and the knee straight. Lean forward until you feel a stretch in the calf. Hold 20 to 30 seconds, 3 times, two or three times a day.

3. Slow heel raises with a towel under the toes

Stand on a step holding a railing with a rolled towel under the toes of the sore foot. Rise over three seconds, pause two seconds, lower over three seconds. Start with both feet if needed, then progress to the sore foot. Aim for 3 sets of 8 to 12 every other day, and reduce range or load if pain rises above mild.

4. Supportive shoes and short walks

Avoid going barefoot on hard floors first thing. Wear shoes with a supportive arch and some cushioning, and break long standing periods with short seated rests.

Physio tip: Do the arch stretch before your feet touch the floor in the morning. It often takes the edge off those first steps.

When to see a physio

If the first-step pain has lasted more than a few weeks, is limiting your walks or work, or you are unsure which movements are safe, a virtual physiotherapy visit can help. A registered physiotherapist can confirm the pattern, teach the right load progression, and check whether footwear, calf flexibility or foot strength need more attention.

Seek same-day medical care, and do not wait for a routine physio slot, if you have:
  • Sudden severe pain after a fall or twist, or inability to bear weight.
  • Swelling with redness and warmth (possible infection or gout).
  • Pain that wakes you at night and does not ease with position change.
  • Heel pain together with other joint swelling and feeling unwell.
In an emergency, call 911.

Frequently asked questions

How long does plantar fasciitis usually last?

Many people notice improvement within 6 to 12 weeks of consistent stretching and strengthening. Full recovery can take several months, and a small percentage need more input if symptoms persist. [1][3]

Do orthotics cure plantar fasciitis?

The 2023 guideline advises against orthoses as a stand-alone short-term treatment. They can help when combined with exercise and education. [1]

Is walking good or bad?

Short, comfortable walks are usually fine once the sharp first-step pain has eased. Sudden long hikes or long periods standing on hard surfaces can flare the tissue, so build up gradually. [4]

Should I ice or heat?

Ice for 10 to 15 minutes after activity can calm a flare for some people. Heat before stretching may feel better if the calf is tight. Neither replaces progressive loading.

When is surgery considered?

Surgery is uncommon. Most guidelines suggest well-supervised conservative care for at least six to twelve months first. [3]
